# Realistic Idle City — Mills / Water Power / Mechanized Workshops Spec v0.1

## Purpose

This document defines the **water-powered and early mechanized production layer** for the game. It bridges the project from:

- agrarian village craft specialization
- animal traction and wagon logistics
- charcoal / bloomery / smithy metallurgy
- roads, drainage, and simple market exchange

into:

- pre-industrial towns with reliable non-animal power
- larger throughput workshops
- more regularized materials
- higher labor productivity with heavier maintenance and siting burdens

This is **not** yet a steam-power document and **not** yet a full machine-tool document. It focuses on the phase where a settlement first learns to turn **flowing water into continuous mechanical work**.

---

# 1. Place in overall progression

## 1.1 Era placement

This layer belongs mainly to the **Pre-Industrial Town** phase, but some precursors appear earlier in an agrarian village and some consequences continue into the early industrial city.

Its role in progression is:

1. hand labor dominates early survival and hamlet life
2. animal power expands hauling and agriculture
3. village workshops specialize by craft
4. **water power allows selected repetitive work to run more continuously and at larger scale**
5. better throughput supports denser settlement, more trade, and more specialized labor
6. line-shafted or centrally powered shops become possible later
7. steam and machine tools eventually remove some site dependence, but only after this stage is understood

## 1.2 Why it matters

Water power is one of the first ways a society escapes the ceiling imposed by human and animal muscle for repetitive rotary or reciprocating work.

In game terms, this stage should feel like a major threshold because it changes:

- labor bottlenecks
- timber processing speed
- grain processing capacity
- consistency of workshop output
- viability of larger settlements
- incentive to control waterways and mill sites
- maintenance and infrastructure burden
- seasonal vulnerability to drought, flood, freezing, and silt

# 2. Core design principles

## 2.1 Water power is site-dependent, not portable

A water-powered system should require:

- the right stream, fall, head, or steady flow
- earthworks or channeling
- millhouse / wheelhouse / turbine housing later
- regular debris clearing
- flood and freeze management
- repair of raceways, gates, bearings, shafts, and buildings

A settlement cannot simply place a watermill anywhere.

## 2.2 Mechanical power is not free

Water power reduces certain forms of labor but increases:

- construction labor
- engineering knowledge demand
- materials demand
- maintenance labor
- failure impact when power stops
- dependence on a single powered node

## 2.3 Not every task should be mechanized at once

The first mechanized gains should come from tasks that are:

- repetitive
n- energy-hungry
- throughput-limited by muscle
- easy to couple to rotary or reciprocating motion
- valuable enough to justify the build and upkeep

## 2.4 Workshops become systems, not benches

Once power is introduced, a workshop becomes more than a shelter and tools. It now depends on:

- drive source
- shafts, gears, belts, cams, cranks, or direct drive
- bearing lubrication
- alignment
- speed control or speed limitation
- safety around moving parts
- trained operators and maintainers

# 3. Water source and site logic

## 3.1 Acceptable power sites

The world generator and settlement planner should recognize several broad classes of water-power sites.

### A. Small stream with modest fall
Use cases:
- small gristmill
- light workshop drives
- seasonal mill

Pros:
- easier to build near early settlement
- lower capital cost

Cons:
- highly seasonal
- vulnerable to drought / freezing / debris
- limited power output

### B. Stronger stream or creek with dependable flow
Use cases:
- village gristmill
- sawmill
- light multi-use mill

Pros:
- better all-season viability
- supports more regular production

Cons:
- more exposed to flood damage
- larger earthworks and stronger structures needed

### C. River-side powerworks with race / dam / weir
Use cases:
- town-scale grist / saw / fulling / pumping operations
- more complex power distribution

Pros:
- highest throughput
- supports multiple workshops or later turbine use

Cons:
- much more capital intensive
- stronger governance requirement
- bigger flood and conflict stakes

## 3.2 Site quality variables

Each candidate power site should be scored by:

- average flow
- seasonal low flow
- spring flood risk
- winter freeze severity
- available head
- bank stability
- silt load
- debris / log strike risk
- distance to settlement
- distance to timber supply
- distance to grain fields / storehouses
- ease of race construction
- ease of crossings / roads
- downstream conflict risk with water users

## 3.3 Site-control realism

A good mill site should become strategically important. It may create:

- transport clustering
- workshop clustering
- water rights / access disputes
- need for roads / bridges / depots
- defense/security importance
- settlement expansion toward the site

# 4. Major water-power system components

## 4.1 Intake and control

Basic components may include:

- intake point
- diversion channel / headrace
- gate or sluice
- settling section if sediment is high
- forebay
- spill path / overflow control

## 4.2 Power conversion hardware

Depending on era and technology level:

- horizontal waterwheel
- undershot wheel
- breast wheel
- overshot wheel
- water turbine later within the same general infrastructure family

## 4.3 Power transmission

Possible transmission forms:

- direct axle drive
- gearing between wheel and stones or shafts
- cranks and rods
- cams for lifting hammers
- belts or ropes later
- line shafts in more advanced shops

## 4.4 Output machines / driven functions

Examples:

- millstones for grain
- up-and-down saw frame
- trip / tilt hammer
- bellows drive
- stamp or ore-crushing action
- fulling hammers for cloth
- pumps for water raising or drainage

## 4.5 Structural and protective works

- millhouse
- wheel pit / wheel housing
- tailrace
- retaining walls where needed
- timber cribbing or stonework
- access path/road
- storage for grain / lumber / spare parts
- maintenance shed

# 5. Water-power technology ladder

## 5.1 Stage A — Simple rotary water use

This stage is mainly a knowledge precursor.

Features:
- direct wheel or simple rotor use
- small scale
- highly local and fragile
- mostly proof of concept and low-output utility

Game effect:
- minor knowledge unlocks
- first water mechanics understanding
- not yet a town-maker

## 5.2 Stage B — Village gristmill

This is the first major water-power institution.

Primary function:
- grain grinding at throughput and consistency beyond hand querns alone

Prerequisites:
- regular grain production
- grain storage
- enough demand to justify dedicated construction
- carpentry and masonry competence
- basic gearing / shaft / bearing know-how
- governance over shared use and maintenance

Effects:
- lower labor cost per food staple processed
- faster flour/meal throughput during harvest season
- stronger settlement centralization
- more dependence on roads, schedules, and maintenance

## 5.3 Stage C — Water-powered sawmill

This is a major construction and trade accelerator.

Primary function:
- converting logs into boards and planks faster and more uniformly than pit-saw or hand-saw work

Prerequisites:
- strong timber supply
- log hauling system
- good mill site
- carpentry and iron-fastening competence
- enough building demand or trade demand

Effects:
- faster housing growth
- easier barn / workshop / bridge / wagon production
- stronger packaging/crate/logistics capacity
- better dimensional regularity in timber stock

## 5.4 Stage D — Specialized workshop drives

Possible powered workshops:
- fulling mill for cloth finishing
- forge bellows drive
- trip-hammer forge
- ore stamping / crushing
- pumping mill

This stage marks the point where power is no longer just “mill grain” but becomes an all-purpose mechanical service layer for the settlement economy.

## 5.5 Stage E — Multi-function power site

At higher pre-industrial maturity, one site may support:

- mill + saw + pumping
- shared race and water-control works
- multiple buildings along a controlled flow path
- more formal schedules and priority rules

This should require strong governance and more advanced maintenance organization.

# 6. Major powered institutions

## 6.1 Gristmill

### Function
- grind grain into meal/flour
- reduce food preparation burden
- support larger population and trade

### Main inputs
- grain
- water flow
- millstone upkeep
- labor for loading, handling, bagging, cleaning

### Main outputs
- meal / flour grades
- bran / coarser fractions depending on process
- dust and waste sweepings

### Realism notes
- stones wear
- stones require dressing/maintenance
- wet grain or contaminated grain causes quality problems
- waiting queues appear during harvest peaks
- storage and rodent control around the mill matter

## 6.2 Sawmill

### Function
- convert logs to boards, planks, beams, slabs, offcuts

### Main inputs
- logs
- water power
- saw maintenance
- hauling labor

### Main outputs
- standardized timber stock categories
- slabs / bark / sawdust / offcuts

### Realism notes
- sawmills create bulky byproducts
- siting near both water and timber transport matters
- wet conditions increase rot and slip risks
- log jams and handling injuries are common hazard families

## 6.3 Fulling mill

### Function
- mechanically finish woven woolen cloth by beating/shrinking/thickening it

### Main inputs
- woven cloth
- water power
- trough/work chemistry appropriate to setting

### Main outputs
- denser, finished cloth suitable for better garments and trade

### Realism notes
- this should matter only once a real textile chain exists
- it is an excellent example of mechanizing repetitive human labor rather than inventing an entirely new product

## 6.4 Water-powered forge support

### Function
- drive bellows and later hammers to improve forge throughput

### Main inputs
- water power
- forge fuel
- metal stock / bloom / billets

### Main outputs
- faster heating cycles
- larger workpieces possible
- more repeatable forging rhythm

### Realism notes
- this does not eliminate skilled smith labor
- it multiplies a trained workshop instead of replacing it

## 6.5 Pumping works

### Function
- raise water for settlement use, industrial use, irrigation assistance, or drainage

### Early uses
- local water lifting
- mine drainage precursors later
- maintaining water distribution at a power site

### Realism notes
- this is a critical bridge from “water source” to “water utility” thinking
- pumping should introduce maintenance-heavy but powerful settlement effects

# 7. Mechanized workshop maturity ladder

## 7.1 Protected work area

At first, workshops are still mostly manual, but they have:
- dedicated sheltered space
- stock organization
- tool storage
- process layout

## 7.2 Manual specialist shop

Now the shop has:
- permanent staff
- dedicated workflow
- higher quality expectation
- regular supply demand

## 7.3 Power-assisted shop

This is the key transition:
- one or more operations powered mechanically
- workshop schedule tied to water conditions or shared drive access
- larger maintenance budget
- more dangerous moving parts

## 7.4 Power-dependent shop

The shop now relies on power for normal throughput.

Consequences:
- outages have real economic cost
- buffer stock matters
- backup manual capacity may still exist but at much lower throughput

## 7.5 Multi-machine power workshop

This is a precursor to later factory logic:
- several machines linked to one power source
- more formal maintenance roles
- load balancing / scheduling
- stronger standards for shafts, belts, bearings, lubrication, and operator discipline

# 8. Mechanical transmission and workshop layout

## 8.1 Transmission families

### Direct drive
Best for:
- small gristmills
- simple single-purpose devices

Pros:
- simpler
- fewer losses
- easier to understand

Cons:
- inflexible
- tied tightly to one machine

### Geared transmission
Best for:
- millstones
- speed/torque adjustments
- more sophisticated multi-step motion

Pros:
- allows better matching of wheel speed to work speed

Cons:
- needs better materials, alignment, and maintenance

### Cam / crank systems
Best for:
- hammers
- reciprocating saws
- pumping

Pros:
- converts rotary motion into repetitive action

Cons:
- wear, impact, and alignment become major concerns

### Belted / line-shafted systems
Best for:
- later mechanized workshops and mills

Pros:
- one power source can feed many machines
- easier to add or rearrange some downstream drives than with all-geared systems

Cons:
- alignment, belt slip, overhead hazard, speed management, and bearing upkeep become major issues

## 8.2 Layout rules

A mechanized workshop should care about:
- dry floor vs wet splash zone
- stock staging near machine entry
- output staging near exit
- safe clearances around moving parts
- maintenance access
- lubrication access
- fire separation where sparks and timber dust coexist
- debris and dust removal

# 9. Water power, labor, and staffing

## 9.1 New roles created

This stage should introduce or strengthen roles such as:
- miller
- millwright
- sawyer
- race keeper / gate tender
- maintenance carpenter
- shaft / gear maintainer
- stone dresser
- log yard handler
- grain queue manager / storekeeper
- water steward / works overseer

## 9.2 Why millwright matters

A millwright should be a distinct high-value trade because this role bridges:
- carpentry
- alignment
- gearing
- power transmission
- water control
- maintenance planning

## 9.3 Labor shift effects

Water power should reduce some manual labor while increasing other forms:

Reduced:
- hand grinding
- pit sawing in some contexts
- hand fulling / repetitive pounding
- some bellows labor

Increased or newly created:
- channel clearing
- dam/weir/race maintenance
- queue and scheduling management
- inspection and lubrication
- replacement of worn mechanical parts
- flood/ice recovery labor

# 10. Inputs, maintenance, and failure

## 10.1 Continuous burdens

Water-powered installations should continuously require:
- debris clearing
- lubrication
- timber/iron repair stock
- alignment checks
- masonry/earthwork inspection
- race cleaning / desilting
- seasonal preparation

## 10.2 Common failure families

### Water-supply failures
- drought / low flow
- frozen race
- blocked intake
- flood damage
- silt buildup

### Mechanical failures
- broken shaft
- cracked gear tooth
- worn bearings
- loose fastenings
- warped timbers
- failed belts / ropes later
- jammed stones or saw carriage issues

### Structural failures
- collapsed bank
- damaged mill dam or weir
- rotted framing
- wheelhouse damage
- tailrace erosion

### Human/system failures
- overloaded queue
- poor maintenance culture
- untrained operator misuse
- stock contamination in wet mill areas
- delayed shutdown during dangerous conditions

## 10.3 Safety hazards

This era should introduce stronger accident risk around:
- entanglement
- crushing / pinching
- slipping on wet floors
- blade and hammer impact
- log handling injuries
- shafting strikes
- structural collapse during floods

# 11. Water-power economics and settlement effects

## 11.1 Throughput effects

Power sites should increase:
- staple food processing throughput
- timber production rate
- cloth finishing capacity
- forge scale
- pumping/utility potential

## 11.2 Secondary effects

These installations should also increase:
- need for roads to the mill
- trade radius
- demand for storehouses
- demand for governance and schedules
- attractiveness of settlement to specialists
- consequences of water-right disputes

## 11.3 Social and political effects

Shared mills should create realistic governance questions:
- who gets access during peak harvest?
- do households grind their own grain or pay/use service labor?
- who pays for repairs?
- who can divert water?
- what happens during drought?
- does the player set priority: food, lumber, forge, pumping?

# 13. Game conversion rules

## 13.1 Do not treat a mill as a flat bonus

A mill should be a **working institution** with:
- location requirement
- shared access logic
- maintenance state
- output quality
- operator skill
- seasonal vulnerability
- queue congestion

## 13.2 Water power should multiply prepared systems

A gristmill does not create grain.
A sawmill does not create logs.
A fulling mill does not create cloth.

Powered workshops should reward settlements that already built the upstream chain.

## 13.3 Mechanization should create dependency

This stage should feel powerful but fragile:
- better output when maintained
- severe disruption when neglected
- strategic value of spare parts and maintenance labor

## 13.4 Settlement transition gate

A settlement should qualify for this layer only when it has:
- stable food surplus
- enough population to support non-food specialists
- carpentry and masonry capacity
- governance/records for shared infrastructure
- access to a viable power site
- road/logistics connection to the site
- enough spare labor to maintain it

# 15. Transition to later systems

This document should feed directly into:

- machine tools / standardization / interchangeable parts
- steam power / line shaft factories
- municipal pumping and utility works
- larger foundries and forge systems
- later town/industrial transport and repair systems

> before modern industry became portable and flexible, societies first had to learn how to build production around fixed, shared, maintenance-heavy power sites.
