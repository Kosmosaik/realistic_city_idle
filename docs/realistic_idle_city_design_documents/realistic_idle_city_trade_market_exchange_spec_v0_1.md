# 1. Purpose

This document defines how **trade, exchange, and markets** should work in the project.

It answers questions such as:

- Why does exchange emerge at all?
- What is the difference between **sharing**, **allocation**, **barter**, **trade**, and **market exchange**?
- When does a settlement become capable of meaningful trade?
- What goods are realistically tradable at different stages?
- What institutions are required for reliable exchange?
- How do transport, storage, quality, recordkeeping, trust, and measurement affect trade?
- How should outsider traders, local exchange, periodic markets, and later permanent market spaces function?

This is not a content pack.  
It is the **rule document** for exchange.

---

# 2. Scope and fit with the existing stack

This spec must fit the already-established project rules:

- one vulnerable NPC at the start
- survival first
- realistic material dependencies
- explicit items, processes, structures, and logistics
- settlement progression from lone survivor to camp to permanent camp to hamlet
- player control through policies, roles, reserves, permissions, and bounded direct orders
- NPCs as embodied people with needs, trust, morale, interests, and skills
- governance, ownership, rationing, sanitation, storage, and hauling already matter before formal markets exist

Therefore, this document treats trade as something that **emerges out of survival surplus, storage, transport, and social contact**.  
It is not an always-on abstract economy from day one.

# 3. Core design position

## 3.1 Trade is not the same thing as allocation

Inside the settlement, much of the early economy is **allocation**, not market trade.

Examples:
- deciding who eats which food
- protecting seed reserve from consumption
- assigning hides to clothing vs straps vs bags
- deciding whether a storage vessel is for water, fat, seed, or cooking
- assigning labor to survival work

That is governed primarily by:
- ownership rules
- communal rules
- reserve policies
- legitimacy and fairness
- player orders/policies
- settlement needs

External trade begins only once the settlement has:
- something it can spare
- something it lacks
- enough organization to move and exchange goods without collapsing survival

## 3.2 Trade is not the same thing as barter

Barter is one mode of exchange.
Trade is the broader category.

Possible exchange modes include:
- gift exchange
- reciprocal obligation
- barter
- quota/tribute style extraction
- debt/ledger exchange
- money-mediated exchange
- contract exchange

Early game should use mainly:
- **gift / reciprocity / obligation** inside the group
- **simple barter** with outsiders
- **ad hoc negotiated exchange**
- **later ledger-based and measured exchange**

## 3.3 Markets are institutions, not just tiles

A market is not merely a building or a UI tab.

A real market requires:
- producers or holders of goods
- buyers or demand
- ways to compare quality and quantity
- some expectation of fairness or enforceability
- movement of goods
- social permission and physical safety for exchange
- standards, customs, or conventions
- at least minimal dispute handling
- enough regularity that participants expect exchange to happen again

This means the game should treat markets as a **settlement institution** built on:
- logistics
- storage
- governance
- trust
- weights/measures or equivalent
- reserve discipline
- communication / information

## 3.4 Trade emerges because self-sufficiency is costly

Trade becomes realistic when the settlement realizes:

- some things are easier to make locally than others
- some things are rare locally but available elsewhere
- specialization raises output
- storage allows deferred sale
- transport expands reach
- outsiders bring techniques, tools, seeds, materials, and animals the settlement cannot easily generate itself

This is why trade should begin before full market-town development, but only modestly.

# 4. Realistic exchange modes by stage

# 4.1 Lone Survivor

A lone starter NPC has **no true market participation** in the economic sense.

Possible exchange modes:
- none
- extremely rare direct encounter
- emergency sharing or coercive encounter
- chance recovery of abandoned goods rather than actual trade

Practical rule:
- do not model a real market economy here
- model only occasional outsider contact, if any

Typical exchangeable things at this point:
- gathered food
- water
- simple tools
- fire
- shelter space
- information
- labor help
- medical/helpful aid

But in most cases, the lone survivor is still too poor, too mobile, and too disorganized to be a stable trader.

# 4.2 Primitive Camp

A primitive camp can support **occasional encounter exchange**.

Typical forms:
- one-off barter with a wanderer
- reciprocal gift exchange
- trading cooked food or water for information or a useful item
- exchanging hide scrap / cordage / gathered plants for a sharper tool, pot shard, awl, needle, or preserved food

Key limits:
- no durable surplus
- weak storage
- no reliable standard units
- high vulnerability to fraud or desperation
- little trust network
- every traded item may be survival-critical

Design implication:
- trade exists, but it is **rare, risky, and small-scale**

# 4.3 Permanent Camp

A permanent camp can support **repeatable outsider exchange**.

Now realistic exchange routes include:
- regular but infrequent visitor
- nearby camp-to-camp barter
- return visits by the same trader/wanderer
- swapping food surplus, hides, baskets, cordage, pots, or good stone for missing goods

This is the first stage where a settlement can start to value:
- specialized raw materials not found locally
- better tool stone
- salt, if available regionally
- already-domesticated juvenile animals or eggs
- crafted needles, awls, hooks, cord, netting, small containers
- information about routes, seasons, hazards, and other camps

Still, trade remains constrained by:
- low surplus
- low transport capacity
- poor roads
- reserve fragility
- quality inconsistency
- weak records

# 4.4 Tiny Hamlet

A tiny hamlet can support **proto-market behavior** even without a formal marketplace.

This means:
- some NPCs start producing more than their own household uses
- some NPCs need tools/materials they do not personally make
- outsider visits become more valuable
- simple bookkeeping becomes useful
- periodic exchange spaces become possible
- pack loads and batch trade become realistic
- storage for trade lots becomes worth building

Typical early hamlet exchange:
- grain or dried food
- fiber, yarn, or cloth lengths
- hides / softened hides / leather-like goods
- baskets / mats / cordage
- clay pots
- charcoal
- worked wood goods
- simple metal items if present
- breeding stock, eggs, manure, seed, breeding rights, or service labor later

This is also the first stage where the settlement may realistically begin to distinguish:
- **household use stock**
- **communal reserve**
- **trade stock**
- **seed stock**
- **tool stock**
- **emergency reserve**

# 4.5 Agrarian Village

This is where real local trade becomes durable.

Typical changes:
- more consistent surplus
- more specialized producers
- more repeated inter-village contact
- more demand for missing specialties
- more need for standard units and quality rules
- more demand for warehouse/storekeeper/bookkeeper roles
- higher value from roads, carts, pack animals, and local market days

A realistic village economy may still be mostly self-provisioning, but it now clearly benefits from:
- periodic market day
- middlemen / traveling merchants
- inter-village specialization
- exchange of scarce craft products and agricultural surpluses

# 4.6 Market Village / Market Town onset

This is the first fully developed target for this spec.

At this point:
- a dedicated market space becomes realistic
- regular exchange schedules matter
- transport radii expand
- storability, grading, and pricing matter more
- professional intermediaries appear
- bookkeepers, weighers, warehouse keepers, and market stewards become legitimate jobs
- the economy begins shifting from “we make what we need” to “we make enough of some things to exchange”

# 5. Why trade emerges in this project

Trade should emerge from multiple realistic causes at once.

## 5.1 Ecological unevenness

Different locations provide different advantages:
- better clay
- better tool stone
- salt access
- better timber
- better grazing
- more stable water
- better fishing
- better metal-bearing ground later
- better soils or longer growing season

No one site should have everything.

## 5.2 Skill unevenness

NPCs differ in:
- interests
- aptitudes
- learned skills
- role fit
- consistency

Therefore even if two settlements have the same raw materials, one may produce:
- better hides
- better pots
- straighter shafts
- better baskets
- better cordage
- more seed surplus
- cleaner preserved foods

## 5.3 Seasonal unevenness

Even similar settlements experience different timing and risk:
- early frost
- drought stress
- flood damage
- seed failure
- good hunt year vs poor hunt year
- local pest pressure

This makes exchange valuable for smoothing shocks.

## 5.4 Scale unevenness

Some processes become much more efficient when done in batches:
- drying and smoking
- pottery firing
- charcoal making
- weaving
- brewing/fermentation later
- milling later
- metal smelting later

Batch efficiency encourages specialization and exchange.

## 5.5 Information unevenness

Trade is not only about goods.
It is also about:
- route knowledge
- hazard knowledge
- seasonal timing
- prices
- outsider contact
- techniques
- seed quality
- disease and raid warnings
- reputation

Information itself should often be a meaningful tradeable asset or side-benefit.

# 6. Exchange forms the game should model

# 6.1 Intragroup sharing

Used primarily within the settlement.

Characteristics:
- governed by ownership/allocation/rationing rules
- not primarily price-based
- legitimacy and fairness matter more than price
- may still create obligation or resentment

Examples:
- communal meal distribution
- assigning a pot to the cook area
- issuing footwear to the most exposed worker
- distributing better tools by role priority

# 6.2 Reciprocal exchange

Two actors exchange with expectation of future balance rather than exact immediate equality.

Examples:
- one camp shares food now and expects hides later
- a traveler is hosted and leaves a tool or information
- one NPC family helps another in harvest and expects labor later

This is very realistic for early low-monetization societies.

# 6.3 Barter

Direct exchange of one good/service for another without money.

Characteristics:
- common early with outsiders
- suffers from matching problems
- valuation is negotiated
- fragile when goods differ greatly in durability or divisibility
- can still use customary rates

Use for:
- early camp-to-camp exchanges
- trader visits before currency-like conventions are established

# 6.4 Ledger or obligation exchange

Exchange recorded as debt, obligation, or stored credit.

Examples:
- “deliver 3 baskets and receive grain after harvest”
- village storekeeper records that a household has claim to a share later
- trader advances a tool now in return for dried fish later

This is a crucial bridge between barter and money.

# 6.5 Commodity-money or agreed medium exchange

Only appropriate later.

Possible forms:
- metal pieces by weight
- grain-credit units
- salt or cloth units in limited contexts
- minted currency much later

This should not appear too early.
The early project should work mainly on:
- barter
- obligation
- measured goods
- recorded claims

# 6.6 Service exchange

Not all trade is goods.

Examples:
- repair work
- carrying service
- guarding
- animal breeding service later
- transport service
- milling later
- teaching or technical help

This becomes important as specialization deepens.

# 7. Preconditions for meaningful trade

A settlement should not be able to support stable exchange until several conditions exist.

## 7.1 Marketable surplus

The settlement must be able to spare something without endangering:
- food reserve
- seed reserve
- water security
- fuel reserve
- clothing minimums
- critical tool stock

Trade should be blocked or sharply discouraged when it endangers survival.

## 7.2 Storage discipline

Goods must survive long enough to trade.

Trade improves dramatically when the settlement has:
- protected dry storage
- pest-protected seed/grain storage
- covered water storage
- hanging/drying storage
- pottery/containers
- warehoused or reserved lots later

Without storage, trade is mostly forced immediate exchange.

## 7.3 Transport capacity

Trade radius is limited by:
- foot carrying
- path quality
- slope
- mud/flood risk
- animal traction later
- carts/wagons later
- escort/safety needs
- perishability during travel

Transport friction should be one of the strongest trade limiters in the game.

## 7.4 Trust and legitimacy

A realistic exchange system needs at least some of:
- repeat interaction
- social witnesses
- known intermediaries
- steward or market authority
- accepted compensation norms
- restitution rules
- fraud penalties

Without these, exchange is slow, suspicious, and expensive.

## 7.5 Standardized quantity and quality

As trade grows, the settlement needs:
- countable units
- standard baskets / jars / bundles / lengths / weights
- agreed grades
- inspection or trust surrogates

A bag of grain, a pot of fat, and a cured hide are not meaningful commodities if nobody knows:
- how much is there
- how good it is
- whether it will spoil
- whether it matches prior promises

## 7.6 Information flow

Prices, demand, shortage, and route conditions matter.

Trade works better when the settlement has:
- current market information
- repeatable market schedules
- known trader routes
- demand knowledge
- quality expectations
- reputation memory

# 8. Realistic stages of market development

# 8.1 Contact exchange

Earliest stage.
No market infrastructure.

Features:
- one-off exchange
- face-to-face negotiation
- no lasting prices
- extreme dependence on trust, need, and desperation
- often mixed with gift or hospitality norms

# 8.2 Repeated outsider exchange

Still no true market site.

Features:
- same outsider returns
- repeated customary rates begin to form
- settlement remembers fair vs unfair traders
- exchange becomes policy-sensitive
- reserve controls matter

# 8.3 Periodic exchange point

This is the first true proto-market.

Features:
- exchange happens at known intervals
- participants expect others to attend
- more than one seller and buyer may appear
- price comparison becomes possible
- social oversight increases
- theft/fraud rules begin to matter more

A tiny hamlet can plausibly host or attend this kind of market before it can support a permanent market district.

# 8.4 Village market

Now the market has some durable features:
- fixed open area
- routine schedule
- stored stock nearby
- designated stall or layout rules
- market steward / guard / bookkeeper
- transaction records
- accepted local units
- some intermediaries

# 8.5 Market town

Now the settlement becomes a regional node:
- warehousing
- market square
- permanent shops/stalls
- periodic fairs and daily trade layers
- tax/fee systems
- specialized intermediaries
- reliable grades/measures
- road and transport support
- integration with governance and administration

# 9. Tradable goods by stage

# 9.1 Primitive Camp / early Permanent Camp

Suitable goods:
- dried food
- smoked food
- gathered plants/herbs
- hides or hide scraps
- cordage
- woven baskets or mats
- useful stone
- small worked bone items
- pottery if firing is consistent
- information
- labor/service
- animal products like feathers or sinew
- rare finds

Unsuitable goods:
- seed reserve unless desperate
- critical water containers
- only cutting tool
- core bedding/clothing in cold conditions
- last fuel reserve
- most fresh perishables over long distance

# 9.2 Tiny Hamlet

Additional suitable goods:
- grain surplus
- protected seed stock only if explicitly designated as tradeable
- clay pots by type/quality
- preserved fats or oils if stable enough
- cloth lengths / yarn / spun fiber
- wooden handles/shafts
- improved baskets
- tanned or softened hides
- eggs, chicks, juvenile domestic stock if acquired
- manure later in local exchange
- charcoal
- soap later
- simple metal goods if present

# 9.3 Agrarian Village and beyond

Additional suitable goods:
- larger grain lots
- flour/meal later
- breeding animals
- textiles
- carpentry goods
- wheels, carts, parts
- iron tools
- nails/fasteners later
- lime, bricks, tiles later
- glassware later
- fuels and lubricants much later
- machine parts later

# 10. Goods that should be hard to trade

Not everything should be equally marketable.

## 10.1 Reserve-protected goods

Trade should be forbidden or heavily penalized for:
- famine reserve food
- protected water emergency reserve
- seed reserve
- essential medicines/care items
- minimal footwear/clothing stock in cold season
- key replacement tool reserve
- breeding pair reserve

## 10.2 Highly perishable or fragile goods

These require:
- short travel times
- specific containers
- specific temperatures
- rapid sale

Examples:
- fresh meat in warm weather
- milk later
- wet hides before preservation
- uncured plant materials
- delicate produce

## 10.3 Identity-critical or governance-critical goods

Some things may require authority to trade:
- communal tools
- official reserve jars
- public seed stock
- public records
- settlement-owned livestock
- strategic weapons if you include them later

# 11. Value and pricing

## 11.1 There is no single “true” value

Value should emerge from:
- need urgency
- scarcity
- season
- transport cost
- spoilage risk
- quality
- substitutability
- labor difficulty
- knowledge intensity
- trust in the seller
- future expectation of shortage or surplus

## 11.2 Early valuation should be approximate, not perfect

In the early game, actors should often value goods through rough categories such as:
- trivial
- useful
- important
- scarce
- strategic
- irreplaceable

Exact numerical prices can still exist internally, but NPC behavior should allow:
- haggling
- mistaken trades
- panic sales
- generous exchanges
- exploitative pricing when trust is low
- better terms when relationships are strong

## 11.3 Later pricing becomes more regular

As markets mature, prices should stabilize more around:
- common units
- standard grades
- seasonal norms
- transport-adjusted differences
- competition among traders

## 11.4 Price shocks

Realistic causes:
- drought
- flood
- road washout
- predator or raid activity
- disease in livestock
- storage spoilage
- market information change
- new trader entry
- bumper harvest
- poor harvest

These should affect not just a number, but behavior:
- hoarding
- earlier sale
- delayed sale
- route switching
- substitution to other goods

# 12. Measurement, grading, and standard units

# 12.1 Why measurement matters

Stable exchange becomes much easier when quantity is legible.

Without that, every trade requires:
- re-counting
- re-estimating
- re-negotiating
- more trust
- more fraud risk

## 12.2 Realistic early standard units

Before formal scales, the game should use practical units like:
- 1 bundle
- 1 basket-load
- 1 pot
- 1 jar
- 1 skin bag
- 1 arm-length
- 1 hide
- 1 shaft
- 1 head of livestock
- 1 day of labor
- 1 fuel stack
- 1 carrying load

These are imperfect but realistic.

## 12.3 Transition to more precise trade

Later, more formal units appear:
- standard container volumes
- weight-based units
- length units
- count by quality class
- moisture or grain quality checks later
- measured cloth lengths
- measured metal weights

## 12.4 Quality grades

Even before precise scales, quality grading should matter:

Examples:
- grain: dry / acceptable / damp / mold-risk
- hide: raw / scraped / dried / smoked / softened / damaged
- pot: porous / serviceable / cracked / cooking-grade / storage-grade
- charcoal: poor / mixed / good
- seed: viable / uncertain / old / contaminated
- cordage: weak / usable / strong
- livestock: healthy / thin / breeding-grade / immature

The more mature the market, the more quality grading affects price and trust.

# 13. Traders, intermediaries, and middlemen

## 13.1 Middlemen are realistic and useful

A realism-first game should not assume all direct producer-to-consumer exchange.

Intermediaries exist because they can:
- travel farther
- aggregate lots
- bear delay risk
- store goods
- grade goods
- spread information
- match buyers and sellers
- provide credit or advance payment
- coordinate transport

## 13.2 But intermediaries can also exploit

Risks include:
- information asymmetry
- monopoly over routes
- unfair weights or measures
- predatory credit
- collusion
- price suppression where producers lack alternatives

This makes market information, local competition, records, and governance important.

## 13.3 Early game intermediary types

- itinerant trader
- peddler
- pack trader
- neighboring camp representative
- specialist craft seller
- settlement-appointed exchange steward
- local aggregator/storekeeper later

## 13.4 Later intermediary types

- warehouse keeper
- broker
- wholesaler
- transport contractor
- market official
- moneylender / credit provider
- merchant house later

# 14. Storage and warehousing

# 14.1 Trade depends on storage

Storage changes trade by allowing producers to:
- wait for a better time to sell
- aggregate enough quantity for worthwhile trade
- protect quality
- smooth seasonality
- separate reserve from trade stock

## 14.2 Basic trade storage in the early scope

Relevant early structures:
- dry basket store
- hanging dry rack store
- sealed or covered jar store
- granary
- cool storage pit for suitable foods
- trade goods shelf / rack / hut
- guarded communal store

## 14.3 Warehouse logic

A true warehouse does more than hold items.
It may provide:
- verification of quantity
- verification of quality
- protection from pests/theft/moisture
- issuance of receipts or records later
- easier aggregation
- easier taxation/fee collection later
- more trustworthy exchange

## 14.4 Seasonal sale pressure

Without warehousing, many producers must sell at harvest, when supply is high and prices are often low.
With storage, they can:
- delay sale
- reduce spoilage
- time exchange
- hold reserve against shocks

This should be an important gameplay driver from hamlet onward.

# 15. Transport, routes, and trade radius

# 15.1 Trade radius is not just distance

It depends on:
- terrain difficulty
- water crossings
- mud/flood
- forest density
- weather
- danger
- carrying method
- pack animal or cart access
- load fragility
- perishability
- route knowledge

## 15.2 Early realistic transport profiles

### Foot porter
- lowest infrastructure requirement
- low volume
- high fatigue cost
- strong perishability penalty
- flexible route choice

### Pack animal
- larger loads
- fodder/water burden
- route width and safety matter
- injury/veterinary risk

### Cart / wagon
- much larger loads
- strong dependence on roads/bridges/clear paths
- weather vulnerability
- huge importance to market expansion

## 15.3 Trade should feel local first

In the early scope, most trade should be:
- neighboring camps
- nearby hamlets
- periodic visiting traders
- modest route lengths

Large-distance trade becomes realistic only as:
- storage
- roads
- security
- transport
- records
- surplus

all improve.

# 16. Information, bargaining, and price discovery

# 16.1 Information changes power

The side with better information often gets better terms.

Relevant information includes:
- current prices elsewhere
- route conditions
- demand spikes
- harvest conditions
- goods quality expectations
- who is desperate
- who has reserve stock
- which trader is fair
- which market day is active

## 16.2 Market information as a gameplay system

The game should track information quality by actor:
- unknown
- rumor
- observed once
- observed repeatedly
- current and trusted
- out of date

## 16.3 Bargaining realism

Bargaining should be influenced by:
- urgency
- personality
- trust
- confidence
- sociability
- role
- prior relationship
- reputation
- language/cultural familiarity if modeled later
- information asymmetry

## 16.4 Public prices vs private deals

Not all exchange should happen at the same rate.

Possible distinctions:
- public market norm
- friend/family rate
- emergency rate
- predatory rate
- high-trust contract rate
- bulk lot rate
- low-quality discount rate

# 17. Credit, debt, and delayed settlement

# 17.1 Early credit exists before formal finance

Credit in the early game can look like:
- “I owe you a basket after harvest”
- “We will return this animal after breeding”
- “Take this grain now, repay in labor”
- “Use my pot and return with dried food”
- trader advances tool in exchange for promised future goods

## 17.2 Why credit matters

Credit allows exchange even when barter fails due to timing mismatch.

It also introduces:
- trust dependence
- recordkeeping needs
- dispute risk
- dependency
- social leverage

## 17.3 Early enforcement methods

Before courts or formal finance, enforcement may rely on:
- reputation
- witnesses
- kin ties
- steward judgment
- future exclusion
- sanctions
- confiscation under governance rules
- refusal of future trade

## 17.4 Predatory debt risk

The system should allow unhealthy dynamics too:
- debt traps
- coercive future pricing
- trader leverage over desperate households
- forced sale due to reserve failure

A realism-first game should not assume all exchange is fair.

# 18. Governance, law, and market order

# 18.1 Markets need rules

A functioning exchange space usually needs rules on:
- where trading occurs
- who may trade
- what may not be traded
- reserve protection
- market hours/days
- stall or space use
- measurement conventions
- dispute handling
- restitution for fraud or theft
- fees or contributions later

## 18.2 Minimal market governance for early scope

A hamlet-level proto-market should at least define:
- protected reserve goods not for casual sale
- market-contact point
- who can authorize outsider entry to storage areas
- who can witness trades
- how disputes are raised
- who keeps records
- whether communal goods can be traded and by whom

## 18.3 Formal market governance later

Later market institutions may include:
- market steward
- weighmaster / measurer
- warehouse keeper
- scribe/bookkeeper
- guard
- fee collector
- inspection/quality officer later
- contract registrar later

# 19. Social and cultural realism

# 19.1 Outsider trust is limited

Small cohesive settlements are often suspicious of outsiders.
That should influence:
- trade willingness
- price offered
- access to sensitive spaces
- need for escort or witness
- preference for known repeat traders

## 19.2 Hospitality and exchange can mix

A traveler may receive:
- water
- food
- shelter
- information
- care

before or alongside explicit exchange.

This should matter because:
- generous treatment can build future trade ties
- exploitation can damage reputation
- helping an outsider may anger some NPCs if reserves are tight
- refusing aid may protect survival but damage longer-term relations

## 19.3 Trade can strengthen or weaken cohesion

Positive effects:
- new goods
- new knowledge
- better morale from variety
- access to missing materials
- stronger inter-settlement ties

Negative effects:
- envy
- hoarding
- favoritism
- corruption
- outsider dependence
- conflict over who gets the benefits
- leadership legitimacy problems

---

# 20. Player control and trade policy

# 20.1 The player should govern trade through policy, not full puppeteering

The player should define:
- whether outsider trade is allowed
- who may negotiate
- which goods are protected
- trade reserve minimums
- acceptable counterpart types
- desired import and export categories
- emergency sale permissions
- debt permissions
- whether communal goods can be exchanged
- whether travelers may be hosted

## 20.2 Core player policies

### Trade openness
- closed
- emergency only
- limited outsider barter
- open local exchange
- scheduled market day
- regular market operations later

### Reserve protection
- forbid sale below reserve
- allow controlled sale above reserve
- emergency liquidation allowed / forbidden

### Counterparty trust policy
- known traders only
- any peaceful outsider
- settlement-approved only
- faction/relationship filtered later

### Price strictness
- accept almost any fair deal in emergency
- normal bargaining
- hold out for stronger deals
- reputation-first even at lower price
- reserve-first even if price is high

### Debt policy
- no debt
- debt only with witnesses
- debt allowed with known traders
- settlement-backed debt only
- household debt allowed / forbidden

### Trade focus
- survival imports
- tool/material imports
- seed and breeding stock
- knowledge and rare goods
- cash-equivalent or medium accumulation later
- export craftsmanship
- export grain surplus
- balanced

## 20.3 Direct orders

Possible direct orders:
- initiate barter with visitor
- refuse all nonessential trade
- hold stock for next market day
- release goods from communal reserve
- escort trader
- send pack train or trade party
- open market square for scheduled day
- close market due to hazard or unrest

Helpful:
- patience

- memory

- fairness
- caution

- curiosity

Risky:
- impulsiveness
- greed
- timidity
- gullibility

- resentment under perceived unfairness

# 22. Market spaces and physical layout

# 22.1 Proto-market layout

At hamlet level, a market may just be:
- open cleared space
- visible and socially supervised
- near but not inside reserve storage
- reachable by path
- close enough to water and rest, but not blocking essential work areas

## 22.2 Basic functional zones

Useful market subzones:
- visitor arrival area
- inspection/meeting area
- exchange/display area
- trade goods staging zone
- witness/record spot
- pack/carry loading area
- overflow/temporary holding area
- public but controlled access path to storehouse

## 22.3 Market safety

The settlement should care about:
- theft risk
- fire risk
- contamination risk around food exchange
- outsider access to children/animals/reserves
- conflict escalation
- weather exposure for goods

# 23. Failure modes and distortions

The trade system should allow realistic problems.

## 23.1 Information failure
- producer does not know normal price
- trader spreads false scarcity rumor
- outdated price information causes bad sale

## 23.2 Measurement failure
- underfilled basket
- cracked pot passed as sound
- wet grain sold as dry
- false count
- tampered weights later

## 23.3 Storage failure
- trade lot spoils before sale
- stored grain molds
- pests destroy high-value goods
- rain damages market stock
- theft from loosely controlled store

## 23.4 Governance failure
- leader trades protected reserve
- steward favors relatives
- witness is corrupt
- outsider bypasses market rules
- sanctions are not enforced

## 23.5 Social failure
- resentment over who may trade
- jealousy over household profit
- conflict over common resources being privatized
- trust collapse after one bad deal

## 23.6 Logistics failure
- route washed out
- animal injured
- porter exhausted
- goods dropped or broken
- delay ruins perishables

# 24. First-playable guidance

Even though this spec reaches into village/market-town logic, the first playable should keep trade modest.

## 24.1 First-playable trade slice

Recommended minimum:
- no standing market on day one
- occasional outsider encounter
- simple barter/gift exchange
- a tiny list of tradable goods
- reserve-protection rules
- settlement memory of fair/unfair outsiders
- later in the slice: repeat trader and a proto-market day

## 24.2 Good first tradable categories
- dried food
- simple pots
- baskets
- cordage
- hides
- sharp stone
- needles/awls/hooks
- seed samples
- already-domesticated chicks/kids/lambs only through realistic routes
- information

## 24.3 Good first trade decisions for the player
- allow / refuse trade
- what is off-limits
- minimum reserve
- who can negotiate
- emergency sale allowed or not
- host traveler or not
- accept debt or not

# 27. Design decisions locked by this spec

1. **Trade does not replace allocation.**  
   Early internal economy remains primarily governed by social and survival rules.

2. **Formal markets should emerge late relative to survival systems.**  
   Lone survivor and primitive camp are not real market economies.

3. **Tiny hamlets may support proto-markets before full market squares exist.**

4. **Trade should depend strongly on logistics, storage, trust, and reserve policy.**

5. **Measurement, grading, and records are major realism gates for mature exchange.**

6. **Middlemen are realistic, useful, and sometimes exploitative.**

7. **Currency is not required for meaningful early trade.**  
   Barter, reciprocal exchange, and ledger obligations are sufficient.

8. **Protected reserve goods must be central to the system.**  
   A settlement that sells seed or famine reserve too casually should suffer.

# 30. Short conclusion

A realism-first trade system in this project should begin **small, local, social, and fragile**.

Early exchange is not a polished market with prices floating in the air.
It is:
- reserve-sensitive
- trust-sensitive
- storage-limited
- transport-limited
- information-poor
- highly shaped by season and necessity

Only after surplus, roads, storage, records, and repeated contact grow should the settlement evolve into a true market village and, later, a market town.

That keeps trade believable, useful, and deeply connected to the rest of the simulation.
