# Realistic Idle City — Machine Tools / Standardization / Interchangeable Parts Spec v0.1

## Purpose

This document defines the **precision-production threshold** where a society moves from:

- skilled craft shops making custom-fitted goods
- repairs by hand fitting and one-off adjustment
- locally idiosyncratic dimensions and patterns

into:

- measured parts
- repeatable dimensions
- gauges and standards
- machine-assisted shaping
- reliable spare parts
- repair as a system
- assemblies that can scale beyond one master craftsperson

This is one of the most important realism gates in the entire project because many late-game systems only become maintainable when parts are **not merely similar, but reliably substitutable within controlled tolerances**.

---

# 1. Place in overall progression

## 1.1 Era placement

This layer begins as a late pre-industrial / early industrial transition and becomes essential in the early industrial city and beyond.

Its direct prerequisites are:
- established metallurgy
- strong fuel chain
- forge and foundry competence
- better timber and structural regularity
- powered workshops
- recordkeeping and measurement culture
- skilled mechanics and toolmakers

Its direct consequences are:
- machine shops
- faster, more reliable repair
- complex assemblies with replaceable parts
- standardized fasteners and shafts
- scalable vehicle and engine industries later
- more formal inspection and quality control

## 1.2 What changes at this threshold

Before this threshold:
- many parts fit only the exact tool or machine they were made for
- repair often means filing, shimming, bending, re-drilling, hand scraping, or reworking on site
- replacement inventories are hard to maintain
- production depends heavily on a few elite artisans

After this threshold:
- parts are produced to defined dimensions
- gauges, patterns, jigs, and fixtures matter
- spare parts can be stocked
- assembly time drops
- maintenance becomes predictable
- larger systems become sustainable

# 2. Core design principles

## 2.1 Interchangeability is not “identical by eye”

Interchangeable parts should be defined by:
- target dimensions
- acceptable variation / tolerance
- controlled mating surfaces
- inspection method
- process capability

Two parts looking similar is not enough.

## 2.2 Standards are social as well as technical

Standardization requires more than tools. It also needs:
- shared drawings or dimension rules
- agreed thread forms / sizes / stock classes
- approved gauges and masters
- inspection routines
- records
- workers trained to make and verify the same thing the same way

## 2.3 Machine tools do not replace craft knowledge

Machine tools should **reorganize and amplify** skilled work, not eliminate skill.
They rely on:
- setup skill
- measurement skill
- tooling choice
- maintenance skill
- fixture design
- material understanding

## 2.4 Precision manufacturing has a hidden support pyramid

A functioning machine-tool layer implies all of the following:
- stable materials
- castings or wrought stock with acceptable consistency
- cutting tools
- abrasives / finishing methods
- lubrication
- power transmission
- temperature awareness
- gauges and standards
- inspection labor
- spare tool and part inventories

# 3. Standardization ladder

## 3.1 Stage A — Functional similarity

At this stage, parts are made “close enough” by skilled workers.

Examples:
- wagon wheel parts sized to local practice
- hinges and straps with local variation
- hand-cut pegs, wedges, pins, blades

Effects:
- acceptable for simple tools and structures
- poor for scalable machine repair

## 3.2 Stage B — Pattern and template production

At this stage, the workshop uses:
- master patterns
- tracing templates
- reference sticks/rules
- repeated hole spacing jigs
- repeated stock lengths and sections

Effects:
- better repeatability
- lower reliance on memory alone
- still significant fitting during assembly

## 3.3 Stage C — Gauge-based fit control

Now the society introduces:
- master parts
- go/no-go style thinking
- fixed gauges
- dedicated checking tools
- accepted dimension ranges

Effects:
- much better consistency
- true spare-part concepts begin to appear
- inspector and tool-room functions emerge

## 3.4 Stage D — Machine-assisted precision

Now machine tools and better measurement systems make repeatable shaping much more practical.

Effects:
- faster production of regular features
- tighter dimensions
- less hand finishing per part
- true interchangeable production becomes plausible in selected product families

## 3.5 Stage E — System-wide standards

This is when standards spread across many shops and products.

Features:
- standard screw threads / fasteners
- standard shaft diameters
- standard stock sizes
- standard hole and fit practices
- shared drawings and part numbering later

Effects:
- repair networks become much stronger
- supplier ecosystems become possible
- complex machinery becomes much more maintainable

# 4. What counts as a machine tool in this game

## 4.1 Definition

For this project, a machine tool is a powered or semi-powered precision-working machine used to shape material — especially metal — in a controlled, repeatable way.

## 4.2 Core families

### A. Lathes
Functions:
- turn cylindrical stock
- cut shoulders, journals, faces
- produce shafts, bushings, threaded work later

### B. Drilling / boring machines
Functions:
- place holes more accurately and repeatably
- enlarge/correct holes
- support better alignment and assembly

### C. Milling machines
Functions:
- produce flat surfaces, slots, controlled profiles
- support more complex regular parts

### D. Planers / shapers
Functions:
- produce true flat surfaces and slots on larger or awkward parts

### E. Gear-cutting / generating equipment later
Functions:
- produce gear teeth with far better consistency than hand methods

### F. Grinding / finishing / abrasive machines later
Functions:
- refine size, finish, and fit

## 4.3 Earlier precursor tools that matter

Before full machine-tool maturity, the following should already matter:
- files
- scrapers
- hand drills / bow drills / brace and bit families
- reamers
- taps/dies later
- vises/clamps
- straightedges / squares / rules
- calipers
- patterns / templates
- simple gauges
- jigs and fixtures

# 5. Precision-enabling systems

## 5.1 Measurement culture

A society cannot get interchangeability just by owning a lathe.

It also needs:
- agreed reference dimensions
- clean readable drawings or measurement instructions
- calibrated rules and gauges
- awareness that temperature, wear, and setup affect fit
- shop discipline around checking work

## 5.2 Gauges

A realistic gauge ladder:
- reference stick / master part
- shop rule and square
- comparison caliper
- dedicated fit gauge
- go/no-go gauge
- master gauge, inspection gauge, working gauge hierarchy later

## 5.3 Jigs and fixtures

These should be treated as huge realism multipliers because they:
- position the workpiece consistently
- reduce operator variation
- shorten setup time
- improve repeatability
- lower skill floor for some repetitive operations

## 5.4 Drawings and part definition

The society should progress from:
- oral or remembered dimensions
- scratched marks and templates
- workshop patterns
- measured sketch/drawing
- controlled drawing set / codified part definition later

# 6. Machine-shop maturity ladder

## 6.1 Repair shop

Main role:
- fix broken tools, wagons, mills, hinges, hardware

Characteristics:
- mostly manual
- some specialized fixtures
- high artisan dependence

## 6.2 Tool room / precision corner

Main role:
- maintain master patterns, gauges, special tools
- prepare jigs and precision pieces for other shops

Characteristics:
- low throughput
- high skill concentration
- strong quality importance

## 6.3 Early machine shop

Main role:
- shape metal stock with powered lathes, drills, or boring equipment
- produce more repeatable shafts, holes, and surfaces

Characteristics:
- strong dependency on power and metallurgy
- setup-heavy
- maintenance-heavy
- still not mass production

## 6.4 Interchangeable-parts shop

Main role:
- produce selected part families to controlled fit

Characteristics:
- gauges and inspection routine
- stock and tool standardization
- reduced hand fitting
- spare parts possible

## 6.5 Production machine shop

Main role:
- support large equipment manufacture and repair networks

Characteristics:
- specialized roles
- repeated part families
- standard screw threads and fasteners
- integrated inspection and records

# 9. How interchangeability should work in the game

## 9.1 Product families, not universal perfection

Interchangeability should first appear in **specific part families**, not across everything.

Examples of early success:
- standard bolts/nuts of a given shop standard
- repeated pulley sizes
- repeated shaft diameters
- wagon axle components within one regional standard
- selected rifle/tool/machine components later if the society reaches that level

## 9.2 Fit classes

A useful game model is to divide parts into fit reliability bands:

- **Custom-only** — almost always needs fitting
- **Template-compatible** — often close, some fitting needed
- **Workshop-standard** — usually fits within one shop system
- **Regional/industrial standard** — fits across many shops using the same standard

## 9.3 Repair effects

Before interchangeability:
- repair time high
- specialist requirement high
- downtime high
- spare inventory weak

After interchangeability:
- repair time drops
- stockable spare parts become valuable
- service economies become viable
- machine uptime improves

## 9.4 Assembly effects

Before interchangeability:
- assembly is a craft bottleneck

After interchangeability:
- assembly becomes more modular
- tasks can be split among more workers
- inspection becomes more important
- scaling production becomes plausible

# 11. Inputs, maintenance, and failure

## 11.1 Hidden requirements

A functioning machine-tool layer should require:
- quality metal stock / castings / forgings
- cutting tools
- abrasives
- lubricants
- power
- alignment and leveling
- spare belts/gears/bearings later
- inspection time
- calibration/gauge upkeep

## 11.2 Common failure families

### Measurement failures
- worn gauges
- inconsistent masters
- poor training in checking
- thermal / setup drift
- unreadable or conflicting drawings

### Process failures
- chatter / poor finish
- misalignment
- wrong cutting speed/feed conceptually
- tool wear
- distortion from bad stock
- poor hole placement / boring error

### Institutional failures
- shops using different standards
- parts mislabeled
- no spare records
- legacy machines with obsolete dimensions
- standards drift over time

## 11.3 Safety hazards

Machine shops should bring new hazard families:
- entanglement in rotating work
- flying chips and swarf
- sharp edges/burrs
- crushing and pinch points
- hot workpieces / friction heat
- oil/slip hazards

# 12. Standard screw threads and fasteners as a special gate

## 12.1 Why threads matter

Standard threads should be treated as a major practical leap because they:
- simplify repair
- simplify inventory
- allow easier assembly/disassembly
- reduce one-off fitting
- make supply chains more compatible

## 12.2 Before standard thread systems
- many fasteners are local, hand-cut, or inconsistent
- replacement is slow
- cross-shop compatibility is poor

## 12.3 After standard thread systems
- bolts and nuts can be stocked in known sizes
- machines can be repaired with stocked fasteners
- downstream products become easier to maintain

# 16. Transition gates

## 16.1 Entry gate to this layer

A settlement should not unlock this phase merely by “research.” It should require:
- significant metallurgy competence
- stable powered workshops or equivalent energy access
- a culture of records/measurement
- enough skilled labor to dedicate to tooling and inspection
- repeat demand for replaceable parts
- enough settlement complexity that downtime is now costly

## 16.2 Exit gate toward later industrial systems

The society should be considered ready for later engine/vehicle/industrial assembly systems when it can reliably provide:
- standard stock classes
- repeatable shafts/bores/threads
- maintained gauges
- stocked spare parts
- trained machinists and inspectors
- multiple workshops working to common standards

---

# 17. Main gameplay thesis

This layer should make one historical truth obvious to the player:

> complex machinery becomes much more sustainable not when the society merely learns how to make a machine once, but when it learns how to make, measure, inspect, replace, and repair its parts predictably.

That is the bridge from craft complexity to industrial complexity.
