# 1. Purpose

This document defines how **craft specialization** and **workshops** should emerge, operate, and expand in the game.

It answers questions such as:

- When does a task stop being a general camp chore and become a trade?
- What makes a workshop a real institution instead of just a recipe station?
- Which crafts realistically appear first?
- What inputs, tools, buildings, hazards, wastes, and support systems do they need?
- How do surplus, agriculture, logistics, governance, and trade change craft work?
- How should workshop roles expand from hamlet to village to market town?

This is not a content pack. It is a **systems-and-structure spec** for craft production, labor specialization, training, workshop siting, and workshop categories.

# 3. Realism principles for craft specialization

## 3.1 A trade appears when repeated work, repeated demand, and repeated know-how meet

A trade should not appear just because a player researched it.

A believable craft specialization requires at least:

1. **repeated demand**
2. **repeated material access**
3. **repeated process knowledge**
4. **enough surplus to free labor from full-time food gathering**
5. **enough space and safety to dedicate a work area**
6. **enough logistics to keep tools, fuel, and inputs flowing**

This fits the broader project rule that production is path-dependent and that every productivity jump depends on solving upstream bottlenecks. fileciteturn78file3

## 3.2 Workshops start as work areas before they become buildings

Historically, many crafts begin as:

- an outdoor or semi-covered work patch
- a hearth or fire setup
- a drying line or rack
- a bench, stump, slab, or floor space
- a pit, kiln, vat, rack, loom frame, or anvil area

Only later do they become:

- dedicated huts or sheds
- enclosed shops
- larger yards with stores and helper space
- specialized workshop clusters
- market-facing production sites

This is important because it avoids unrealistic leaps from “no craft” straight to “fully built profession house.”

## 3.3 Specialization is not just efficiency; it is a social and logistical burden

Every specialist adds dependency pressure:

- they must be fed
- their tools must be maintained
- their work area must be supplied
- their outputs must be stored or exchanged
- their wastes and hazards must be managed
- their training must be reproduced if they fall ill, die, or leave

## 3.4 Most preindustrial workshops are material-bound and site-sensitive

Crafts often cluster near their dominant constraints.

Examples:

- potters near clay, temper, and fuel
- tanners near hides, water, bark/tannin, but downwind from housing
- smiths near charcoal, ore/metal stock, water for quenching, and hauling routes
- carpenters near timber yards and seasoning racks
- weavers near fiber supply, spinning labor, and dry protected interiors
- millers at reliable power or grain concentration points

## 3.5 Workshop realism depends heavily on mundane support systems

FAO material on village processing emphasizes that small-scale processors depend on adequate raw material supply, suitable processing space, fuel or power, maintenance/servicing, transport, and technical support. citeturn186690search2turn778101search2turn778101search6

That means a believable workshop in your game must care about:

- stock continuity
- dry storage
- breakage and waste
- cleaning
- ventilation
- fire risk
- operator fatigue
- skill transfer
- repairs
- transport distance

# 4. What “craft specialization” means in gameplay terms

A craft specialization exists when the colony has crossed from:

> “anyone can occasionally do this badly”

to:

> “specific NPCs now perform this work repeatedly, in a fitted space, with better tools, better knowledge, better output quality, and ongoing support needs.”

A craft can therefore exist at several maturity levels:

## 4.1 Informal craft

- occasional task
- crude tools
- variable output
- no dedicated worker
- no dedicated shop
- mostly self-use

## 4.2 Proto-specialized craft

- one NPC does it more often than others
- dedicated area or shelter exists
- small stock of inputs/tools exists
- output sometimes supports others
- some knowledge is retained and teachable

## 4.3 Established workshop craft

- one or more NPCs hold the role regularly
- a real workshop building/yard exists
- inputs, fuel, and output have designated storage
- output quality and reliability improve
- the trade contributes to settlement viability or exchange
- apprenticeship or supervision becomes meaningful

## 4.4 Market-facing specialist craft

- production exceeds internal demand at times
- standard measures and quality classes matter
- records and stock accounting matter
- repair/rework workflows matter
- trade dependency starts shaping priorities

## 4.5 Mechanized or institutionally scaled craft

- power source or advanced tools transform output
- sub-specialties appear
- inspection and standardization intensify
- workshop becomes part of a larger supply chain

# 5. Preconditions for workshop emergence

## 5.1 Material preconditions

A workshop cannot stabilize unless its key inputs are reasonably obtainable.

Examples:

- **pottery**: clay, temper, water, fuel, drying space
- **weaving**: fiber/wool, spindles, loom frame, dry sheltered workspace
- **tanning**: hides, scraping tools, drying structure, fats/oils or tannins, water, waste area
- **smithing**: metal stock or ore path, charcoal/fuel, forge heat, hammering/anvil surface, quench water, tongs/tools
- **carpentry**: timber supply, seasoning/stacking space, edged tools, sheltered bench area

## 5.2 Settlement preconditions

A workshop becomes plausible only after a settlement has enough of the following:

- food reserve above day-to-day emergency minimum
- some fuel reserve
- protected tool and material storage
- enough hauling labor
- enough shelter to free some labor from emergency survival work
- governance or accepted rules about stock use and role allocation

## 5.3 Social preconditions

Specialization works better when:

- the settlement recognizes the role
- the specialist has priority access to necessary tools/materials
- the community accepts that the specialist will not spend all day gathering food
- the specialist’s output is protected from random consumption or misuse
- trainees or backups can be assigned later

## 5.4 Knowledge preconditions

The knowledge/discovery stack matters here.

A workshop should usually require some combination of:

- discovery knowledge of materials
- procedural knowledge of repeated steps
- tool knowledge for jigs, fixtures, or improved equipment
- quality knowledge to spot failures
- safety knowledge to reduce accidents and waste

# 6. Main drivers of specialization

## 6.1 Surplus

Without surplus there is no durable specialization. This is the foundational rule from the base design. fileciteturn78file1

## 6.2 Repetition

The more often a settlement needs the same output, the more value there is in a dedicated worker and fitted workspace.

## 6.3 Material difficulty

Tasks that require fragile timing, heat control, pattern memory, or subtle hand skill tend to specialize sooner.

## 6.4 Tool cost

The more expensive or scarce the tools, the more they tend to cluster around dedicated workers and shops.

## 6.5 Hazard management

Processes involving smoke, sparks, sharp tools, bad odors, contamination, or wastes often get pushed into more formal spaces.

## 6.6 Trade opportunity

Once exchange begins, crafts that can create durable, transportable, tradable goods become more attractive.

## 6.7 Repair burden

A settlement with more tools, carts, harness, looms, pots, doors, and farm equipment creates an internal repair economy. Repairs often drive specialization before new production does.

# 7. Workshop maturity ladder by settlement stage

# Stage A — Late Permanent Camp

## 7.1 Identity

This is still mostly multifunctional labor, but repeated work areas begin to appear.

## 7.2 Typical proto-crafts

- hide scraping/softening/smoking
- cordage making
- basket weaving
- simple tool shaping/hafting
- clay shaping and simple firing
- food drying and smoking
- simple sewing/repair using bone awls and sinew/fiber

## 7.3 Workspaces

- scraping frame
n- drying rack
- smoking frame
- clay pit and firing spot
- simple covered bench or work mat
- hanging storage and repair corner

## 7.4 Labor pattern

The same NPCs still rotate among food, hauling, and craft tasks, but a few begin to show stronger interest and aptitude in particular tasks.

## 7.5 Main realism point

At this stage there are no true “shops” in the later sense. There are only emerging craft zones.

# Stage B — Hamlet / First Surplus Economy

## 8.1 Identity

This is where the first true proto-specialists appear.

The hamlet now has fields, storage, and role separation starting to emerge. fileciteturn77file0

## 8.2 Typical hamlet specialists

- cook/preserver
- hide worker
- clay worker/potter
- spinner
- weaver (very early, often still blended with spinning and sewing)
- builder/repairer
- toolmaker
- seed keeper/storekeeper

## 8.3 Hamlet workshop types

### A. Fiber and textile shelter

Functions:
- drying fiber
- storing cleaned fiber
- spinning
- warp setup
- weaving under dry shelter
- mending garments and bags

Why it appears:
- clothing and sacks become recurring needs
- weaving requires order, dryness, and protection from dirt and moisture
- loom setup benefits from fixed space

Britannica notes that weaving relies on a loom that holds warp threads in order, and loom-based weaving is one of the oldest organized textile techniques. citeturn778101search0turn778101search4turn778101search8

### B. Pottery yard

Functions:
- clay cleaning and tempering
- vessel shaping
- drying under controlled conditions
- loading pit fire or kiln
- sorting intact vs cracked ware

Why it appears:
- containers become strategic for water, seed, grain, fats, fermentation, and cooking
- clay work benefits from repeated technique and batch scheduling

Pottery is one of the oldest useful crafts and depends on careful drying before firing; early firing could be done in pits before later kilns became common. citeturn428396search5turn186690search0turn186690search6

### C. Hide and leather area

Functions:
- fleshing/scraping
- stretching/drying
- softening/oiling/smoking
- storage of usable leather-like material
- cutting straps, wraps, and patches

Why it appears:
- hides are bulky, smelly, perishable, and process-sensitive
- footwear, straps, bags, and clothing become recurring needs

Britannica’s tanning/leather coverage supports treating hide preservation and tanning as multi-stage processes involving preparation, tanning/preservation, and finishing rather than a single recipe step. citeturn186690search1turn186690search4

### D. Builder/carpentry patch

Functions:
- shaping poles and timbers
- cutting stakes and handles
- shelter repair
- fence and rack building
- simple furniture and fixtures

Why it appears:
- settlement growth creates constant repair and fixture demand
- wood shaping is easier and safer when tools and stock stay in one place

## 8.4 Hamlet rule for specialization

No hamlet specialist should be fully exempt from survival labor all year. Seasonal fallback work remains normal.

# Stage C — Agrarian Village / Basic Craft Specialization

## 9.1 Identity

This is the first stage where society begins differentiating strongly into trades. That is already explicit in the base design. fileciteturn78file2

## 9.2 What changes here

The village can now support:

- year-round specialists in some trades
- higher-value tools and equipment
- larger work yards and sheds
- more specialized outputs
- regular repairs for farm expansion
- better trade readiness

## 9.3 Core village workshop families

## 9.3.1 Carpentry and joinery

### Role
Carpenters shape timber into buildings, storage fixtures, handles, doors, frames, carts, and agricultural supports.

### Why carpentry becomes central
The village increasingly relies on:
- framed buildings
- carts and wheel parts
- ladders
- bins and storage fixtures
- press and loom frames
- animal housing components
- plow beams and handles

The original design already places carpentry and joinery at the center of agrarian-village development. fileciteturn78file2

### Typical inputs
- poles
- seasoned timber
- planks/boards later
- pegs and wedges
- fiber or leather lashings
- glue later
- iron fittings later

### Typical tools
- axes/adzes
- wedges and mauls
- knives and drawknives
- augers/borers later
- saws later
- chisels later
- measuring cords and straight edges

### Workshop needs
- dry wood storage
- seasoning racks
- covered bench space
- shaving/scrap area
- adjacency to timber yard and haul routes

### Main outputs
- handles and shafts
- poles and stakes
- doors/gates
- stools/chests/shelves
- loom frames
- carts/wagons later
- wheel blanks and hubs later

### Risks/wastes
- splinters and cuts
- tool dulling
- large waste piles and fire risk
- warped lumber if stored badly

## 9.3.2 Spinning, weaving, and textile finishing

### Role
This trade turns plant or animal fiber into yarn, cloth, sacks, wraps, thread, and basic clothing.

### Why it specializes
Textile production is labor-intensive and sequential:
- harvest/collect fiber or wool
- clean
- dry
- separate/card/comb
- spin
- warp
- weave
- cut/sew/repair

Britannica notes the close historical relationship between basketry and weaving and the central role of the loom in organized cloth production. citeturn778101search4turn778101search8

### Typical inputs
- flax-like plant fiber, nettle, bast fiber, wool, hair
- spindle whorls/spindles
- loom frame parts
- dyes later
- sewing needles/awls

### Workshop needs
- dry enclosed or semi-enclosed area
- protection from mud and rain
- hanging/storage space for yarn and warp
- seating/lighting appropriate for fine handwork

### Main outputs
- yarn
- rough cloth
- sacks and bands
- garments and wraps
- repair patches
- ropes/belts from thicker cordage branch

### Risks/wastes
- mildew damage from dampness
- tangled warp and wasted time
- contamination by dirt, grease, smoke, or pests

## 9.3.3 Pottery and clay products

### Role
Potters create storage, cooking, transport, and later building products.

### Why it matters
Containers multiply survival and village capacity:
- water storage
- grain storage
- seed storage
- cooking vessels
- fermentation vessels
- lamps later
- tiles/bricks later

### Inputs
- clay
- temper
- water
- shaping tools
- firing fuel

### Workshop needs
- clay processing zone
- drying shelves
- covered drying space
- separate firing area
- shard dump/recycling pile

### Outputs
- bowls and jars
- lids/stoppers
- cooking pots
- seed jars
- storage crocks
- tiles/bricks later

### Risks/wastes
- cracking in drying/firing
- fuel consumption
- fragile transport
- smoke and heat hazard around kilns

## 9.3.4 Hideworking, tanning, and leatherwork

### Role
This workshop family turns perishable hides into usable materials for clothing, straps, bags, footwear, harness parts, and later saddlery.

### Why it specializes
Hide processing is dirty, odorous, water-using, and process-sensitive. It is also critical once animal traction and more durable gear become important.

### Inputs
- raw hides/skins
- scraping tools
- stretching frames
- smoke/fat/oils
- bark or other tannin sources later
- water

### Workshop needs
- downwind siting
- separation from water source and food handling
- wash/soak area where appropriate
- drying and smoking structures
- waste pit and runoff control

### Outputs
- soft hide
- tougher leather-like stock
- straps/thongs
- pouches and bags
- footwear pieces
- belts and harness pieces later

### Risks/wastes
- strong odor
- contamination of nearby water/food areas
- insect attraction
- large waste load from flesh/hair/offcuts

Britannica’s tanning coverage supports the long, staged nature of leather production and the importance of tanning materials such as bark-derived tannins in many historical methods. citeturn186690search1turn186690search4

## 9.3.5 Charcoal burning

### Role
Charcoal burners convert wood into a higher-quality fuel for heat-intensive crafts.

### Why it matters
Charcoal is cleaner-burning and can support higher, more controllable temperatures than raw wood, making it strategically important for metalwork, some ceramics, and lime burning. The base design already identifies charcoal as a major transition fuel. fileciteturn78file2

### Inputs
- suitable wood
- stacking space
- earth/covering materials for clamps or pits
- ignition and monitoring labor

### Workshop/yard needs
- large wood yard
- firebreaks
- downwind siting
- haul access
- covered charcoal storage after production

### Outputs
- charcoal
- ash/fines

### Risks/wastes
- fire escape
- smoke burden
- labor lost if burn goes wrong
- deforestation pressure if unmanaged

## 9.3.6 Smithing / forge work

### Role
Smiths repair and produce metal tools, fasteners, fittings, and later more complex components.

### Why it specializes
Smithing demands concentrated heat, practiced hammer control, stock management, and a dedicated safe workspace.

Traditional blacksmithing uses forge heat, hammering, and centuries-old tool/fuel techniques to turn iron or steel into useful objects. citeturn428396search15turn428396search0

Britannica’s bloomery overview supports the iron path that precedes more advanced furnace systems: iron is reduced from ore with charcoal into a bloom that then requires forging and working. citeturn778101search1

### Inputs
- charcoal
- ore/bloom/iron stock/scrap
- flux or auxiliary materials depending on stage
- quench water

### Tools
- forge/hearth
- anvil surface
- hammers
- tongs
- punches/chisels/files later
- bellows later

### Workshop needs
- open or well-ventilated forge area
- spark-safe floor and surroundings
- charcoal store
- metal stock rack
- quench bucket/trough
- repair bench

### Outputs
- knives and blades
- nails and rivets later
- hinges and hooks
- adze/axe heads
- plow fittings later
- hoops and tires for wheels later
- repair work for many other crafts

### Risks/wastes
- burns
- eye injury from scale/sparks
- high fuel consumption
- fire risk
- toxic smoke if poor fuels/materials are used

## 9.3.7 Lime burning and masonry support

### Role
Lime burners and masons make more durable construction and sanitation possible.

### Why it specializes
Once the village starts wanting more durable foundations, plaster, waterproofing assistance, and improved masonry, lime becomes important. The base design already ties kilns and lime to stronger masonry, plaster, sanitation structures, and waterproofing improvements. fileciteturn78file0

### Inputs
- limestone or shell where regionally plausible
- fuel
- water for slaking later
- sand/aggregate later

### Outputs
- quicklime/slaked lime depending process stage
- plaster/mortar inputs later

### Risks/wastes
- hot caustic material
- smoke and fuel burden
- siting/fire control needs

## 9.3.8 Soap and cleaning-material production

### Role
Soapmakers turn fats and alkali ash into cleaning materials that support hygiene and workshop maintenance.

### Why it matters
Soap is not just a luxury; it improves cloth cleaning, workshop cleaning, and some health outcomes indirectly. The base design already frames it that way. fileciteturn78file2

### Inputs
- animal fat/oil
- ash-derived lye or alkali source
- water
- boiling vessel

### Workshop needs
- controlled boiling area
- careful labeling/storage
- child/food separation due to caustic materials

### Outputs
- soft soap or basic soap stock
- cleaning solutions

### Risks/wastes
- caustic burns
- contamination if used in food area
- fuel use

## 9.3.9 Wheelwright, wagon, and harness support

### Role
These related specializations emerge once animal traction, roads, and heavier hauling matter.

### Why they specialize
Wheels, hubs, axles, vehicle frames, and harness gear require repeated repair and fitting. They are too important to remain purely ad hoc once transport capacity becomes central.

### Inputs
- timber and shaped wood stock
- leather/harness material
- iron tires/fittings later
- grease/lubricants later

### Outputs
- wheels
- hubs/spokes/rims
- cart frames
- yokes and harness components
- ongoing transport repairs

### Dependencies
- carpentry
- leatherwork
- smithing
- roads/logistics
- animal management

## 9.3.10 Milling and power-assisted crafts

### Role
Millers and millwrights appear where grain volume and water/power opportunity justify them.

### Why this is a later workshop family
The base design already places mills at the agrarian village / pre-industrial threshold and notes that waterwheels could power milling, sawing, pumping, forge bellows, hammers, and textile operations. fileciteturn78file0

Britannica identifies watermills as early machines, and mills in the Middle Ages usually referred to water-powered grain processing infrastructure. citeturn560782search3

### Outputs
- milled grain/flour/meal
- powered sawing later
- powered bellows/hammering later

### Dependencies
- stable grain surplus or timber throughput
- suitable site
- construction skill
- maintenance labor

# 10. Transition from village craft to market-town workshop economy

## 10.1 What changes at market-town level

The base design already states that scale and coordination begin to matter more than bare survival, and that workshops become linked to roads, storage, regular exchange, and role-based labor schedules. fileciteturn78file0

At this stage, workshops begin to change in six big ways:

1. **more reliable input contracts or trade channels**
2. **more regular output beyond household need**
3. **greater role separation within a craft family**
4. **more stock accounting and measurements**
5. **more repair, grading, and standardization**
6. **more dependence on outside demand and price conditions**

## 10.2 New or stronger market-town crafts

- fuller/dyer
- baker
- brewer/fermenter where setting supports it
- butcher
- glazier/proto-glass worker
- brick/tile maker
- cooper
- saddler/harness maker
- sawyer
- millwright
- wagon repairer
- measured-goods storekeeper

Colonial and early town craft clusters often included many of these trades in one settlement because local production, repair, storage, and exchange were tightly interdependent. Historic Bethlehem’s Colonial Industrial Quarter gives a good example of one place where numerous trades coexisted together, including spinning, weaving, cooperage, wheelwrighting, saddlery, flax processing, carpentry, masonry, bakery, candlemaking, and more. citeturn428396search10

## 10.3 First institutional craft organizations

Without jumping straight to formal guild systems, the game can start representing:

- master worker / helper / apprentice structures
- quality expectations
- output reservations for internal survival needs before export
- common measures and named product grades
- dispute handling over bad workmanship or stock misuse
- repair priority queues

Britannica notes that craft guilds emerged as towns grew and division of labor became more pronounced. That is useful grounding for later town-stage organization, even if the early game remains looser and more informal. citeturn778101search3turn560782search4turn560782search1

# 11. Workshop siting doctrine

Every workshop record should include siting rules.

## 11.1 High priority siting variables

- proximity to raw material source
- proximity to fuel source
- proximity to water if needed
- separation from potable water if polluting
- separation from sleeping areas if noisy, smoky, or smelly
- proximity to haul routes and yards
- drainage and flood safety
- fire safety buffer
- room for drying, seasoning, or waste piles

## 11.2 Typical siting patterns

### Near housing / center
- sewing and mending
- spinning and weaving
- small food processing
- tool repair bench
- fine handwork

### Edge of settlement
- pottery firing
- charcoal burning
- lime burning
- smithy
- brick/tile works
- slaughter and heavy waste zones

### Downwind / separated / controlled runoff
- tanning
- slaughter/butchery at larger scale
- soaking pits
- waste-heavy boiling/processing

### Route-facing
- carpentry yard
- wagon repair
- smithy servicing tools and transport
- warehouse-linked workshops

# 13. Workshop staffing model

## 13.1 Minimum staff states

Each workshop should support these staffing modes:

### A. Unstaffed but extant
- building/yard exists
- tools and fixtures exist
- no active specialist currently assigned
- may decay or be used only ad hoc

### B. Single specialist
- one primary operator
- low throughput
- vulnerable to illness/exhaustion
- can train a helper slowly

### C. Specialist + helper
- better hauling/setup/cleanup support
- more continuous throughput
- better task switching and maintenance

### D. Specialist + apprentice
- some throughput penalty at first
- knowledge transfer improves resilience
- training quality depends on supervisor skill and patience

### E. Multi-worker team
- only plausible once demand, logistics, and space justify it
- more common in market-town and beyond

## 13.2 Support roles often matter more than the craft role itself

A workshop may fail not because the craftsperson is bad, but because:

- charcoal does not arrive
- wood is wet or unseasoned
- hides rot before processing
- fiber storage mildews
- clay cracks while drying in bad weather
- stock is misallocated
- roads are too muddy to move timber
- food reserves force the specialist back into field labor

This is exactly the kind of realism your game benefits from.

# 14. Training, apprenticeship, and replication of trades

## 14.1 Why this matters

A craft should not be a magical permanent unlock just because one NPC once did it successfully.

A settlement needs:

- the worker
- the tools
- the worksite
- the material flow
- the procedural memory
- the ability to teach or recover the practice if interrupted

## 14.2 Training stages

### Observer
- can fetch, watch, sort, clean, and assist
- gains vocabulary and process familiarity

### Helper
- performs setup/cleanup/material handling
- learns sub-steps

### Apprentice
- completes supervised steps
- high error rate but faster growth

### Competent craft worker
- can run standard work alone
- still struggles with edge cases

### Master or high-skill specialist
- higher yield, lower waste
- better repair and troubleshooting
- can improvise and teach
- can judge quality and redesign workflow

Historic craft communities and modern historic-trades programs alike rely heavily on apprenticeship and guided mastery rather than pure written instruction. UNESCO’s treatment of traditional craftsmanship and Colonial Williamsburg’s historic trades both reinforce that craft transmission is deeply practical and embodied. citeturn428396search4turn778101search3

# 15. Quality, variation, and failure

Craft outputs should not be binary.

## 15.1 Quality drivers

- worker skill
- worker fatigue and morale
- material quality
- tool condition
- workshop condition
- weather and humidity where relevant
- fuel quality and temperature control
- time pressure
- supervision quality

## 15.2 Typical failure modes by craft family

### Carpentry
- crooked pieces
- bad fit
- split joints
- warped members

### Weaving/textiles
- weak yarn
- broken warp
- uneven fabric
- mildew/contamination

### Pottery
- collapse during shaping
- cracking during drying
- underfiring/overfiring
- breakage in handling

### Leather/hides
- rot before processing
- stiffness or brittleness
- poor preservation
- contamination/odor complaints

### Smithing
- poor weld/join
- bad temper or edge quality later
- warped pieces
- excess fuel loss

### Lime/soap/boiling crafts
- caustic handling accidents
- incorrect ratios
- weak product or ruined batch

## 15.3 Repair and rework

A mature workshop should often produce:

- first-quality output
- second-quality output
- salvageable output
- scrap/waste/byproduct

Repair and rework should be major parts of workshop gameplay.

# 16. Hazards, sanitation, and workshop health burdens

## 16.1 Workshop hazards are part of realism, not side notes

Examples:

- smoke inhalation
- carbon monoxide risk in enclosed combustion
- burns and scalds
- sharp-tool injuries
- repetitive strain
- chemical/alkali burns from soap/lime workflows
- contaminated runoff from tanning or slaughter-linked work
- mold risk in textile and fiber spaces
- fire spread from kilns, forges, and charcoal clamps

## 16.2 Workshop cleanliness is trade-specific

Not all workshops need the same standard.

### High cleanliness importance
- food processing
- water vessel production/storage handling
- textile finishing and clothing repair
- medical/care adjunct work later

### High contamination and odor burden
- tanning
- slaughter-linked processing
- heavy ash/soot craft
- waste-heavy boiling or soaking processes

### High fire burden
- smithy
- kiln yard
- charcoal site
- lime burning
- candle/grease boiling later

Workshop zoning should therefore interact strongly with hazard, health, and governance specs.

# 17. Craft specialization and trade

## 17.1 Internal vs external outputs

Each workshop should classify outputs as:

- survival-critical internal goods
- internal support goods
- reserve or buffer goods
- tradable surplus goods
- prestige or optional goods later

## 17.2 Typical early tradable craft outputs

- pots and jars
- woven sacks and cloth bands
- cured hides or straps
- charcoal in modest amounts
- repaired tools
- wooden fixtures and handles
- soap/cleaning stock later
- wheel and cart repairs later

## 17.3 Market pressure changes craft behavior

Once exchange expands, workshops start caring more about:

- repeatability
- volume
- timing and delivery
- measurement and grading
- stock reservation
- repair guarantees or reputation
- not consuming seed, fuel, or critical internal reserves for sale

This matches the existing trade doc logic that markets depend on storage, measures, records, and transport discipline rather than spontaneous barter magic.

---

# 18. Craft specialization and governance

Governance matters because workshops often create disputes over:

- access to scarce stock
- priority use of fuel
- ownership of tools
- who gets repair priority
- whether goods may be traded away
- nuisance burdens like smell, smoke, and runoff
- apprenticeship obligations
- product quality disputes

A realistic colony should therefore be able to define at least:

- who may withdraw workshop stock
- who may use workshop tools
- whether a craft shop serves communal need first
- where dirty or dangerous workshops must be placed
- whether apprentices can be assigned by policy or consent rules
- who inspects or signs off on critical outputs later

This taxonomy is meant to guide future regular design docs and later content packs.

## 19.1 Primary craft families

1. **woodcraft**
   - carpentry
   - joinery

   - coopering
   - sawyer work

2. **fiber and textile craft**
   - cordage
   - spinning
   - weaving
   - sewing
   - dyeing/fulling later

3. **hide and leather craft**
   - hide preservation
   - tanning/tawing/oiling branches
   - cobbling later
   - harness and saddlery later

4. **clay and ceramic craft**
   - pottery
   - brick/tile
   - kiln operation

5. **fire and fuel craft**
   - charcoal burning
   - lime burning
   - soap/candle boiling branches

6. **metal craft**
   - bloomery/smelting path later
   - smithing
   - farriery later
   - foundry/machining much later

7. **food-processing craft**
   - preservation
   - baking
   - brewing/fermentation
   - milling
   - butchery
   - dairying later

8. **construction craft**
   - builder
   - mason
   - plasterer
   - roofer
   - brickmaker later

9. **transport-support craft**
   - wheelwright
   - wagon maker/repairer
   - harness maker
   - pack/frame maker

10. **service and maintenance craft**
   - sharpening/repair
   - tool maintenance
   - workshop maintenance
   - inspection/quality control later

# 21. First-playable relevance vs later expansion

## 21.1 Relevant to the early playable slice

Even though this document reaches beyond the first hamlet, only a subset should matter immediately.

For the early slice, the important workshop families are:

- hide work
- cordage/basketry/fiber prep
- pottery
- cooking/preservation
- simple sewing/repair
- basic builder/carpentry patch
- storekeeper/seedkeeper support functions

## 21.2 Relevant after the early slice stabilizes

Once village growth begins, the next wave should be:

- spinning and weaving as stable roles
- true carpentry shed
- charcoal yard
- smithy
- tanner/leather workshop
- wheelwright and harness branches
- lime and masonry support
- milling

## 21.3 Relevant after market formation

- baker/brewer/butcher
- glazier or proto-glass
- cooper
- brick/tile works
- sawmill and millwright work
- stronger quality grading and accounting

# 22. Design conclusions

The correct realism model for craft specialization in this game is not:

> “unlock building, assign worker, receive bonus.”

It is:

- repeated needs create repeated work
- repeated work creates proto-specialists
- proto-specialists create fitted work areas
- fitted work areas create workshops
- workshops create output reliability, repair capacity, and social dependency
- surplus, logistics, governance, and trade determine whether those workshops survive and scale

That keeps the game faithful to the main design idea:

> civilization grows because people, materials, tools, knowledge, and institutions become coordinated — not because a menu says they are unlocked.
